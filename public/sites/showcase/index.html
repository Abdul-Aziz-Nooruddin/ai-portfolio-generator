01

A Short Essay on Fluid Interfaces

Great software feels less like a tool and more like a material — something you press against and it answers. For the past years I have worked at the seam between design and engineering: prototyping shaders before wireframes, measuring easing curves like heartbeats, treating every interface as a small performance that unfolds at sixty frames per second.

My practice sits on three legs. Real-time graphics — GPU particle systems, custom render pipelines and physics simulations that run in the browser, no plugins, no excuses. Interface engineering — design systems and component architectures that survive contact with real teams and real deadlines. And generative craft — algorithms that produce identities, motion and form, so a brand can breathe instead of posing for screenshots.

“The best interface is the one you stop noticing — because it behaves exactly the way the physical world already taught you.” — Working principle № 1

The ink drifting behind this page is not a video. It is a Navier–Stokes fluid simulation computed live on your GPU — velocity advected through a grid, vorticity confined, pressure solved by Jacobi iteration, roughly two hundred times per second you move your cursor. That is the standard I hold my work to: nothing decorative that could not also be engineered honestly.

03

Technical Philosophy

P·01

Prototype in Shaders

If a feeling can be expressed as a fragment shader, write it before drawing a single rectangle in Figma.

P·02

Budget the Frame

Sixteen milliseconds is a contract. Every effect must justify its cost or make room for one that does.

P·03

Motion With Meaning

Easing is grammar. Movement explains hierarchy, causality and state — otherwise it is noise.

P·04

Honest Materials

No fake videos pretending to be realtime. If it moves, it should be simulated, measured and shipped.
